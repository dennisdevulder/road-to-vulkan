The Geometry of Light: Why Vulkan Asks for 900 Lines

Before any code — the four pillars that make every VkStruct stop feeling arbitrary.

I sat down to render a spinning cube — a graphics-programmer rite of passage — and got slammed by 900 lines of Vulkan boilerplate before a single pixel lit up. So I parked the syntax and went looking for the blueprint underneath. This is what I found.

🎧
NotebookLM podcast · 30:11
The Geometry of Light
A NotebookLM-generated deep dive that takes my notes, the frustration with that 900-line Vulkan tutorial, and reverse-engineers the why behind every struct.

Notes

The 900-line wall

Modern Vulkan tutorials don't greet you with drawCube(). They open with VkApplicationInfo, VkInstanceCreateInfo, validation layers, physical device enumeration, queue families, swapchains — a complete wall of code, all of it before a pixel lights up. Older APIs hid the machine behind a friendly state machine; modern APIs expose the raw, unvarnished reality of the silicon. The tutorials hand you a wrench and a 900-page manual on how to tighten one bolt, but they never show you the engine.

What follows is that engine — described in terms of the four physical and mathematical realities every Vulkan struct ultimately maps back to.

Pillar 1 — Geometry: why everything is a triangle

Every landscape, every photoreal character, every spinning cube is, at the bottom, a list of triangles. Three vertices always define exactly one plane — there is zero topological ambiguity. A quad has one vertex too many: nudge it a fraction off and the GPU has to interrogate the shape to figure out which way the crease folds. Triangles remove that decision entirely, so the rasterizer never has to guess.

The "but isn't that twice as much data" worry is real, and it's solved by indexed drawing. Instead of sending six vertices for two triangles of a square face (with two duplicates), you send four unique vertices plus a small index buffer that says draw a triangle from 0,1,2; then another from 0,2,3. Mathematical certainty without the memory tax.

Pillar 2 — Transformation: the five spaces a vertex lives in

Raw coordinates in isolation mean nothing. A vertex at (1, 1, 1) could be the corner of a cube, the tip of a sword, or anywhere in a city. Before it can become a pixel, it has to travel through five coordinate systems:

  • Model space — the object's private universe, centered on its own origin.
  • World space — the shared scene, where the cube sits next to the tree on the table.
  • View space — the world rotated and translated so the camera sits at the origin, looking down z.
  • Clip space — projected through the lens; out-of-frustum vertices are deleted here to save work.
  • Screen space — the physical pixel grid of the monitor.

The W component (the trick that makes 3D work)

A 3×3 matrix can scale and rotate a point but it cannot translate it. So computer graphics promotes every vertex from (x, y, z) to (x, y, z, w) — homogeneous coordinates — and uses 4×4 matrices throughout. Translation works cleanly when w = 1.

The projection matrix does something almost sneaky with w: it copies the vertex's z-depth into it. Then the hardware does a perspective divide — x/w, y/w, z/w. An object far away has a large w, so dividing pulls its x,y toward zero, shrinking it toward the center of the screen. That single division is perspective foreshortening.

The MVP matrix

The Model, View, and Projection matrices are pre-multiplied on the CPU into one 4×4 master — the MVP — and handed to the GPU. The GPU applies it to every vertex in parallel. To animate a spinning cube, the camera and projection don't move; you just rebuild the model matrix with a tiny extra rotation each frame, recompose MVP, and send. That is the entire essence of 3D animation.

Pillar 3 — Hardware: why a CPU can't do this and a GPU can

A CPU has 16–32 fast, complex cores designed for low-latency branching: operating systems, AI pathfinding, physics. Hand it 10 million vertices to multiply by a 4×4 matrix sixty times a second and the frame rate collapses. A GPU is the opposite trade-off: thousands of slower arithmetic units in a SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) layout. One instruction — multiply this vertex by this matrix — fans out across thousands of threads at once.

The pipeline that runs on top of that hardware has three programmable / fixed stages worth holding in your head:

  • Vertex shader — your tiny program, executed once per vertex in massive parallel. Its job: take a vertex, apply the MVP, output a clip-space position.
  • Rasterizer — fixed-function hardware, no shader code. It scans each triangle's bounding box, decides which pixels lie inside via edge functions, and computes barycentric coordinates for each one. A pixel in the dead center of a triangle is 33% / 33% / 33% across its three vertices; near a corner, it's weighted toward that corner. Those weights interpolate every per-vertex attribute — color, UVs, normals — across the surface for free.
  • Fragment shader — runs once per covered pixel. At 4K@60 that is ~500 million invocations per second. It receives the rasterizer's interpolated values, samples textures, evaluates lighting, and outputs a final color.

Pillar 4 — API philosophy: why Vulkan is verbose on purpose

OpenGL felt easy because the driver was secretly doing the work. You'd bind a shader, change blending, hit draw — and behind the scenes NVIDIA's or AMD's driver was frantically intercepting every state change, recompiling shader microcode on the fly, guessing what hardware paths to route through. That hidden CPU-heavy babysitter is the source of frame stutter, vendor inconsistency, and unpredictable performance.

Vulkan deletes that babysitter. Switching blend modes can mean re-routing data through different physical silicon, so Vulkan refuses to do it on the fly. Instead, VkGraphicsPipelineCreateInfo forces you to bake the vertex shader, fragment shader, depth rules, and blend state into a single immutable VkPipeline object — you are pre-programming the physical pathways of the GPU. At draw time the driver does zero guesswork.

Same logic for the rest of the noise:

  • Render passes — a contract describing which color/depth attachments exist, how they're loaded at the start, used by subpasses, and stored at the end. Tile-based GPUs (mobile, Apple Silicon) use it to keep data in on-chip cache as long as possible before flushing to RAM.
  • Memory management — you query the device for memory heaps, find one that's DEVICE_LOCAL (sits on VRAM), allocate a big block, and sub-allocate buffers and textures yourself.
  • Synchronization — CPU and GPU run asynchronously. VkSemaphores and VkFences are how you guarantee the GPU is done reading a buffer before the CPU writes to it for the next frame.
  • Validation layers — the core API does zero error checking at runtime to save cycles. You opt into validation in development to intercept your commands and warn you before you crash the GPU.

OpenGL is an automatic transmission. Vulkan is a manual transmission with the dashboard ripped off and direct control of the fuel injectors and spark plugs. The pain is the price of predictable performance at the bleeding edge.

The lingering thought

The cognitive load on a graphics programmer has never been higher. As scenes get more complex — path-traced photorealism, neural rendering, metaverse-scale simulations — the plumbing keeps getting denser. Maybe the eventual destiny of an explicit API like Vulkan isn't humans writing it at all: a human directs the high-level art, lighting math, and spatial logic; an AI compiler emits the 900 lines of memory allocation and synchronization underneath, optimizing SIMD pathways in a way no human ever could. Until then — we navigate the plumbing ourselves.

The Kitchen: Vulkan's Objects, Mapped to Real Life

Fifteen intimidating vocabulary words become twelve obvious steps in a restaurant — once you stop thinking of draw() as handing someone a marker.

If episode 1 was the math underneath Vulkan, this one is the furniture. Every dense Vulkan term — VkInstance, VkDevice, VkSwapchainKHR, command buffers, fences — maps to a physical step in opening and running a high-end restaurant kitchen. Once the analogy clicks, the API stops looking arbitrary.

🎧
NotebookLM podcast · 22:03
The Kitchen Analogy
A NotebookLM-generated walkthrough of Vulkan's object model, from VkInstance to VMA, told as the story of opening a restaurant kitchen.

Notes

Stop thinking draw() hands a marker to a computer

The naive expectation is draw a triangle, fill it red, move on. Vulkan demolishes that. It's like being asked to sign a lease, hire a management team, build a supply chain, and define the chemical composition of the ink before you're allowed to touch the whiteboard. That's why a "hello triangle" tutorial costs you a thousand lines of code.

The way through is to swap analogies. Vulkan is not a marker. Vulkan is a restaurant kitchen. Every term in the API is a physical step required to get a plate of food out to a customer.

1. VkInstance — registering the business

Creating an instance is going down to City Hall and registering your restaurant. You introduce your application by name and version, declare which Vulkan extensions you'll need ("I'd like to draw to a window, please"), and shake hands with the Vulkan loader on the machine. One per application. First thing created, last thing destroyed.

2. Physical device — the building

The instance lets you query the world for physical devices — actual GPU silicon. A laptop might have two: an integrated chip for browsing, a discrete card for gaming. Vulkan refuses to guess which one you want. You enumerate them, inspect their capabilities, and pick the building you want to put your kitchen inside.

3. Logical device — signing the lease

The physical device is read-only — you can't change the silicon. The logical device is your specific, configurable session with that hardware. The reason this split exists is multi-tenancy: a game, a browser, and Discord can all run at once, each with its own logical device, all pointing at the same physical GPU. You query the physical world; you configure and destroy the logical session.

4. Queues & queue families — the prep stations

Old APIs treated the GPU as one giant single-file line. Modern GPUs have billions of transistors — making a complex math job wait behind a simple pixel job is a colossal waste. Vulkan exposes specialized parallel lanes:

  • Graphics queue — drawing geometry.
  • Compute queue — heavy general-purpose math.
  • Transfer queue — copying big chunks of memory around.
  • Presentation queue — actually putting images on the screen.

When you sign the lease (create the logical device), you must declare the prep stations you intend to use. Even if a beginner tutorial just grabs one graphics queue, the system forces you to acknowledge the kitchen has distinct parallel stations. Nothing happens by accident in this environment.

5. Surface — the window to the dining room

Vulkan's universe is mathematically pure. Your operating system is chaos: window managers, taskbars, focus rules. The surface is the bridge between them — and it's the only platform-specific part of the entire API. There's a different extension for Windows (VK_KHR_win32_surface), macOS, Linux. Once the surface is created, every other call is OS-agnostic. Vulkan stops caring; it just sees a void it's allowed to push pixels into.

6. Swapchain — rotating plates

If you draw straight to the visible image while the monitor is mid-refresh, you get tearing — the top half of the screen shows the old frame, the bottom half shows the new one. Spin the camera in a shooter and the wall looks sliced in half.

The swapchain is a rotating set of two or three images. The chef plates onto plate #1, the waiter carries plate #2 to the table, plate #3 is in the dishwasher. Swap them out behind the scenes; the user only ever sees a finished frame.

7. Command pools & command buffers — order tickets

Vulkan is a recording API, not an immediate-mode one. You never tell the GPU draw a triangle right now. The waiter doesn't walk in and grill the burger themselves — they write a ticket: two burgers, medium-rare, hold the onions, side of fries. That ticket is a command buffer.

Tickets come from a command pool, and you have one pool per CPU thread. That means many waiters can write tickets in parallel — a superpower the old single-thread bottleneck never had.

8. The pipeline — the frozen recipe

This is the most uniquely Vulkan concept of all. A pipeline is every piece of state needed to perform a draw call — vertex shader, fragment shader, vertex layout, blend state, depth test, stencil — baked into one immutable object at startup.

Why freeze it? In OpenGL, developers flipped switches all frame: turn on blending, draw water, turn off blending, draw rock. Easy for the developer, but every flip forced the driver on the CPU to pause and re-validate the whole state combination. Vulkan validates the pipeline once; switching pipelines at runtime is virtually free. The chef doesn't stop and wonder how to make the dish — they execute a pre-validated recipe blindly.

9. Render passes (and the 1.3 shortcut)

A render pass historically described which images the draw call writes into, whether to clear them at the start, what to do with them at the end. Useful metadata, but a pile of paperwork. Vulkan 1.3 added dynamic rendering, which skips most of that boilerplate. The community's pro tip for anyone learning today: use dynamic rendering — it saves hundreds of lines.

10. Synchronization — semaphores & fences

The CPU and GPU run independently. Without explicit timing, the GPU can try to draw a frame the monitor is still displaying, or the CPU can overwrite an order ticket the chef hasn't read yet. Two primitives keep the chaos in check:

  • Semaphores — GPU-to-GPU signals. They live entirely on the graphics card. Example: don't present this image to the screen until rendering is finished.
  • Fences — GPU-to-CPU signals. Tiny flags in system memory. The CPU watches the flag; when the GPU flips it, the CPU knows the frame is done and it's safe to record the next ticket.

This is what enables frames in flight — typically two. The CPU records the commands for frame N+1 while the GPU is still drawing frame N. They overlap, neither sits idle. Each frame in flight needs its own command buffer, its own fence, and its own semaphores.

11. Buffers & memory — the pantry

A buffer is a reserved chunk of GPU memory — vertex positions, indices, uniforms (like the MVP matrix from episode 1). You allocate it, fill it, bind it before you draw. Uniform buffers hold per-frame data the shader reads — typically the camera transforms.

Vulkan exposes the entire memory hierarchy: GPU-only memory, CPU-visible memory, host-coherent memory. Doing it manually means querying the hardware for memory types, allocating large blocks, sub-allocating tiny chunks per resource, binding them one by one. It's tedious and error-prone, and the unanimous community pro tip is do not write your own allocator — drop in the Vulkan Memory Allocator (VMA) on day one. AMD wrote it, everyone uses it. It's the one place in Vulkan where the community agrees to let a tool handle the magic.

The whole kitchen, in one breath

The application says hi to the system and registers the business — the instance. It looks at the hardware and picks a building — the physical device. It signs the lease and opens its session — the logical device. It declares the parallel prep stations — the queues. It builds a window to the OS — the surface. It gets a stack of rotating plates so the user never sees tearing — the swapchain. It hands its multi-threaded waiters pads of paper — command pools — so they can write order tickets — command buffers. The chefs follow a strictly frozen, driver-validated recipe — the pipeline. Chaos is contained with semaphores (GPU↔GPU) and fences (GPU↔CPU). And every raw ingredient lives in the pantry — buffers, ideally allocated by VMA.

The lingering thought

Vulkan's extreme verbosity is a philosophy: forcing every rule, state, and dependency to be explicit up front eliminates bottlenecks at runtime. It strips the illusions and makes you understand the machine you're commanding instead of trusting a driver to guess. Worth asking: where else in our digital lives — or in our day-to-day organisations — are we relying on hidden magic, unwritten rules, black-box systems we should probably rip out and manage explicitly, even if it costs us a much bigger vocabulary to do it?

The Spinning Cube: Building One End-to-End

Eight vertices, 36 indices, eight lines of shader — and a seven-step heartbeat that never changes whether you're rendering a cube or a dragon.

If episode 1 was the math and episode 2 was the kitchen, this is the dish. Walking through every layer of plumbing needed to put a single rotating rainbow cube on screen — and noticing that the same architecture, unchanged, scales all the way to AAA games.

🎧
NotebookLM podcast · 22:38
The Spinning Cube
A NotebookLM-generated walkthrough of building a colored, rotating 3D cube in Vulkan from absolute scratch — geometry to shaders to the per-frame heartbeat to the gotchas that bite every developer at least once.

Notes

The same loop, all the way up

The spinning rainbow cube is the rite-of-passage demo, but it's not a toy. The plumbing required to put it on screen is the exact structural foundation of a photoreal AAA game. Once you can render a cube, the only thing standing between you and a dragon is more data — not more architecture.

1. Geometry — eight vertices, 36 indices, an IKEA box

A cube has eight corners — eight vertices, each at ±0.5 on x, y, z to keep it centered and one unit wide. The GPU doesn't draw squares, only triangles, so each face is two triangles → 12 triangles → 36 corners. Sending 36 vertices would be wasteful: most are duplicates of those 8 unique points, and a vertex isn't cheap (often 32+ bytes once you include color, normals, UVs).

The fix is the index buffer. Like an IKEA flat-pack: ship the four unique table legs once, plus a small instruction sheet that says attach leg 0 here, leg 0 there, leg 1 here. You store eight vertices in memory and a list of 36 small integers (2 or 4 bytes each) pointing back into them. The bigger win isn't memory — it's the GPU's post-transform vertex cache: when an index repeats, the GPU reuses the already-transformed result instead of running the vertex shader again. Free performance.

2. Free gradients (the rasterizer earns its keep)

Give each of the eight corners a different color. The faces don't render as solid blocks — they render as smooth gradients, no extra code. The rasterizer, fixed-function silicon on the GPU, automatically interpolates per-vertex attributes across every covered pixel. A red corner, a blue corner, and a green corner give you a perfectly blended triangle for free.

3. Shaders — eight lines of logic, total

You write tiny GPU programs in GLSL, then compile them to SPIR-V for Vulkan. For this cube you need exactly two:

  • Vertex shader (~6 lines) — multiply the incoming 3D position by the MVP matrix to land in clip space, pass the vertex color through to the next stage.
  • Fragment shader (~2 lines) — receive the rasterizer-interpolated color, output it.

That's the entire logic of a rotating, lit, gradient-shaded 3D cube. Every other line of code in the project is plumbing that delivers data to those eight lines.

4. Buffers — three flavors of GPU memory

The shaders are useless without data. Three buffers feed them:

  • Vertex buffer — the 8 unique corners (position + color).
  • Index buffer — the 36-entry instruction sheet.
  • Uniform buffer — the MVP matrix, refreshed every frame as the cube rotates.

The loading dock (staging buffers)

The CPU and GPU have separate memory architectures, and the CPU can't write directly into the fastest GPU-only memory. So static data (vertex, index) goes through a staging buffer: at startup you fill a slow CPU-visible buffer, then the GPU forklifts it once into ultra-fast device-local memory. Slow once, blazing fast forever after.

Why one uniform buffer per frame in flight

The MVP matrix changes every frame, so you can't bury it behind a staging copy — you allocate the uniform buffer in CPU-visible memory and overwrite it directly each tick. But if there are two frames in flight (CPU recording frame N+1 while the GPU is still drawing frame N), one uniform buffer is a recipe for tearing: the CPU's new matrix would land in the same memory the GPU is mid-read. Solution: one uniform buffer per frame slot. They never step on each other's toes.

5. Descriptor sets — layout, pool, set

Shaders find their uniform buffers through descriptor sets. The restaurant analogy makes the three pieces obvious:

  • Descriptor set layout — the menu. The schema. "Slot 0 will be a uniform buffer; slot 1 will be a texture."
  • Descriptor pool — the kitchen. The big allocator that holds all raw ingredients.
  • Descriptor set — the plated meal handed to the shader for one specific frame: here's the exact uniform buffer you ordered, perfectly portioned.

6. The seven-step heartbeat (every Vulkan app, ever)

An animation isn't a pile of data — it's a tightly orchestrated loop running 60 or 144 times a second. Internalize these seven steps and you've internalized every Vulkan render loop ever written:

  1. Wait on a fence — make sure the GPU is done with this frame slot's resources from the last cycle. Don't paint on a wet canvas.
  2. Acquire the next swapchain image — get a clean canvas from the rotating stack.
  3. Update the uniform buffer — recompute the MVP matrix for this fraction of a second and write it.
  4. Reset and record the command buffer — begin render pass, bind the pipeline, bind the geometry, bind the descriptor set.
  5. Issue the draw call — execute the instruction manual to draw 36 indices.
  6. Submit to the graphics queue — gated by semaphores so the GPU doesn't draw before the canvas is ready.
  7. Present — gated by another semaphore so the swapchain doesn't push to the monitor before drawing finishes. Advance to the next frame slot, loop.

Fences vs semaphores, refined

Two synchronization primitives, two distinct jobs. Fences are the assembly-line workers yelling up to the manager's office — strictly GPU→CPU. Semaphores are the workers handing a clipboard to the next station on the line — strictly GPU→GPU. Neither one is a substitute for the other.

7. Survival tips — gotchas that bite everyone at least once

  • The Y-axis flip. Most math libraries (GLM and friends) assume +Y is up; Vulkan's clip-space convention has +Y pointing down. Use the standard projection matrix as-is and your cube renders perfectly — upside down. Every tutorial mentions it; every developer forgets it at least once. Manually flip the Y term in your projection matrix.
  • No depth testing → MC Escher cube. Without an explicit depth buffer, the GPU draws triangles in submission order with no concept of "in front." Back faces overwrite front faces and the inside of your cube ends up on the outside. Create a depth (Z) buffer — same dimensions as the framebuffer, storing distance instead of color. The GPU rejects any pixel further than what's already there.
  • Window resize → hard crash. The swapchain reserves a specific block of GPU memory for a specific resolution. Drag the window edge and that block doesn't grow — Vulkan throws VK_ERROR_OUT_OF_DATE_KHR and your app dies. You have to catch it, pause the loop, destroy the swapchain, and recreate it at the new size.
  • Validation layers are not optional. By default Vulkan prioritizes raw speed over safety. Forget to wait on a semaphore and you get a black screen with zero diagnostic information — just silent failure. Validation layers attach a strict auditor in development; mistakes turn into explicit human-readable messages. You cannot reasonably ship a Vulkan app without them.

The lingering thought

The architecture you build for a spinning cube is the same architecture a AAA studio runs. Want textures? Pass one in through a descriptor set, add one line to the fragment shader. Want a million-vertex dragon? Same vertex and index buffers, just more data. The seven-step heartbeat never changes shape.

Which raises a question. If the core engine is essentially solved — if the only difference between a rainbow cube and a hyperreal sunset is volume of artistic data — then maybe the next bottleneck for graphics isn't writing better engines. It's how fast we can generate the ocean of geometry, textures, and animation that this loop is hungry for.

How the Vulkan Plugin Actually Works

What problem the plugin solves, how Vulkan reshapes the old OpenGL path, and why macOS is the hard one — the shape of the thing, no method names.

A second GPU plugin for an old MMO that already has one — built on Vulkan instead of OpenGL. This is the conversational map: what a GPU plugin does, why "explicit" is the whole story, how OSRS geometry becomes triangles, and where the Mac-only bugs live.

🎧
Podcast · two voices · ~28 min
How the Vulkan Plugin Actually Works
HOST is the curious learner; DEV built the plugin. No argument types, no class names — just the shape of the thing and the order things happen in.

Notes

Why a second GPU plugin?

Nothing was broken. The existing plugin works great — but it's built on OpenGL, and OpenGL is a museum piece now. Apple deprecated it years ago; the future on every platform is Vulkan, Metal, or DirectX 12. This is a bet: rebuild the renderer on Vulkan, the modern, explicit, cross-platform API, and learn what that actually takes. Explicit is the word that runs through everything.

What a GPU plugin does

The game can draw itself on the CPU — software rendering, pixel by pixel — but it's slow and caps out. A GPU plugin intercepts that. The engine hands us a description of the world ("a tree model at this tile, terrain here, a goblin facing this way") and we hand it to the graphics card. The plugin is a translator: game says "tree here," plugin says "GPU, draw these triangles." Both old and new plugins do exactly this — same input, same output, wildly different machinery in the middle.

OpenGL is a waiter; Vulkan is a kitchen you run yourself

OpenGL is a restaurant with a waiter — the driver. You set a state ("blending is on now") and it stays on until you change it. A big mutable machine that remembers. Vulkan hands you the kitchen keys: nothing is remembered for you. Before the game starts you pre-build frozen recipe cards for every settings combination — pipelines. At draw time you don't set state, you slap down the right card. More work upfront, but it kills the whole "I left a toggle on three draws ago" category of bug.

The three faces of "explicit"

  • Explicit state — pipelines. Bind the card, get exactly that behavior, nothing leaks in from before.
  • Explicit commands — you record a list in a command buffer (a notebook): bind the terrain recipe, draw these zones, switch to the UI recipe. Write the whole to-do list, then hand the notebook to the GPU in one go. The GPU sees the whole plan and schedules it intelligently. Re-recorded every frame.
  • Explicit timing — the part that bites everyone.

Explicit timing: fences and semaphores

The CPU writes the notebook; the GPU reads it — at the same time, on different chips. We keep about three frames "in flight." The trap: frame six wants to write new tree positions into a buffer the GPU is still reading for frame five. Overwrite mid-draw and you get garbage, flicker, crashes. Two signals fix it:

  • Fence — GPU tells the CPU "I've finished this batch, reuse its memory." The leash that stops the CPU running too far ahead.
  • Semaphore — GPU tells itself "step A done, step B can start." Pure GPU-to-GPU ordering; the CPU never sees it.

Fence: GPU→CPU. Semaphore: GPU→GPU. In OpenGL the driver did all this invisibly, with guesses. Vulkan strips the guessing out and makes you say it — more rope, more control, more ways to hang yourself.

OSRS geometry becomes triangles

The engine hands us scene and camera. We walk the world — a 104×104 tile grid — and pull each thing's raw geometry: points in space, triangles connecting them, colors. Each point packs into a tiny 20-byte nugget (position, color, texture coordinate), streamed into a big buffer. Smaller vertex → more fit in cache → faster the GPU chews through them, and there are a lot per frame.

Transparency: the hard part

Opaque stuff is easy — the depth test keeps whatever's closest. See-through things (water, fire) must be blended back-to-front, in order, or the blending is visibly wrong. So a dedicated piece sorts a model's faces by distance before emitting. The game adds a wrinkle: faces carry a "priority" tag, the engine's hand-tuned hint ("this cape part draws over the body even if the math disagrees"), and the sorter respects it — it's matching the original game's look, not textbook depth sorting. Shortcut: a fully-solid small model skips sorting entirely.

Draw the world, then paste the UI on top

Record the notebook: bind the terrain recipe, set camera math and fog, then go layer by layer, plane by plane — terrain, walls, objects — drawing only zones close enough to be visible. Beyond fog distance, we simply never mention it (cheapest culling there is). Roofs vanishing indoors is handled right in the draw list: we know which stretch of buffer is the roof and draw around it — up to it, skip it, resume. The interface is blunt: the game already drew the 2D UI into a block of pixels; we upload that block as one image and stretch it across the screen as a flat transparent layer on top. Scene first, then one UI sheet. Submit, signal, present.

The two shaders

Two little programs that run on the GPU. The vertex shader runs per point: multiply raw position by the camera matrix to find where on screen it lands, work out fog from distance. The fragment shader runs per pixel: figure the final color (model's own or a sampled texture), blend in fog, optionally apply color-blindness correction, output a pixel. Same tiny program, run across thousands of cores in parallel — "write the recipe for one, the hardware does all." That's the GPU in a sentence.

Getting a window to draw into

The GPU needs a surface — a real region of a real OS window. The game's window belongs to Java's old toolkit, AWT, so step one is always: reach through Java, grab the native handle, wrap it in something Vulkan recognizes. The bridge is the same everywhere; the handle differs.

  • Windows — ask Java for the HWND, wrap it in the Windows-flavored surface. Two steps.
  • Linux — grab a display connection and drawable, wrap those. Even on Wayland, Java presents itself as X11, so one path covers both.
  • macOS — its own segment.

macOS: Vulkan that isn't Vulkan

Apple doesn't support Vulkan at all — they have Metal. So a Vulkan plugin runs on a Mac through MoltenVK, a translation layer that rewrites every Vulkan call into Metal underneath. One codebase runs everywhere; macOS pays a translation tax. Worse, the translator has its own personality — bugs that don't exist on Linux show up on Mac. The flicker bug is exactly this: same source, flawless on Linux, flickers on Mac, because the bug isn't in our Vulkan, it's in how the translator hands finished frames to the Mac's compositor.

And macOS needs extra setup just for a surface. Metal needs a CAMetalLayer, which can't be created from Java — it's an Apple system object. So a small chunk of native Objective-C reaches into the Java window, mints a fresh Metal layer, and staples it into the layer stack. Windows and Linux "borrow the existing handle"; macOS "manufactures the handle in a different language." There's a constant babysitting cost too — on resize the Metal layer doesn't follow automatically, so that native code runs a heartbeat tied to display refresh to keep its size in sync. macOS is easily half the platform complexity of the whole plugin.

The abstraction layer, and why it exists

Raw Vulkan is brutally verbose — creating one frozen recipe card means filling out a dozen forms (rasterize, blend, depth-test…). Do that inline every time and the rendering logic drowns. So a thin layer wraps the ceremony behind sane builders, modeled after WebGPU. The win is readability and a clean seam: platform plumbing (instance, device, swapchain, surface) on one side, "what to draw" logic on the other as plug-in extensions. The stock scene renderer is just one extension; someone could add an HD lighting renderer without touching the plumbing. The bonus features ride that seam too — FSR upscaling (render at 75%, upscale back to sharp), a live debug overlay, per-layer wireframe, more anti-aliasing and texture knobs.

The whole thing in five sentences

  1. The plugin turns the game's "here's the world" into triangles the graphics card draws.
  2. Vulkan makes you say everything out loud that OpenGL guessed — frozen pipelines instead of mutable state, a recorded command list instead of barked orders, explicit fences and semaphores instead of trusting the driver to wait.
  3. Each frame: capture geometry, sort the see-through stuff back-to-front, record the draw list zone by zone, paste the UI on top, submit, present — three frames pipelining through three slots so neither chip stalls.
  4. To draw anywhere, borrow the OS window's native handle and wrap it as a surface — trivial on Windows and Linux, and on macOS manufacture a Metal layer in native code and run it through MoltenVK.
  5. That translator is the price of one codebase running everywhere, and it's exactly where the Mac-only bugs live.

Where to start reading: the frame loop — wait on a fence, record the list, submit, present. That's the spine; everything hangs off it. Then geometry capture into the buffer, then the sorter, then surface and swapchain, and save the macOS native layer for last. Don't read the Vulkan setup top to bottom — start where the per-frame action is and pull the threads from there.

The Deep Dive: Down to the Bytes

For someone who finished episode one and wants to open the source without drowning — the 20-byte vertex, reverse-Z, the bucket sorter, push constants, and the genuinely cursed platform landmines.

The map said "capture geometry into a buffer." The territory is: one vertex is exactly twenty bytes in a very specific order, and one wrong byte turns the world inside out. Every concrete detail comes with the why — a junior remembers the reason, not the number.

🎧
Podcast · two voices · ~38 min
The Deep Dive
Down to the bytes, the sync primitives, the sort algorithm, and the cursed platform bugs — still taught, not dumped.

Notes

What one vertex actually is — twenty bytes

Every point of every model becomes a twenty-byte record, in three chunks. First 8 bytes: position — x, y, z as signed 16-bit shorts plus two bytes padding. Why shorts? The world's coordinates are integers already (tiles are 128 units, everything snaps to a grid); a short holds ±32,000, half the size of a float. Next 4 bytes: color and flags packed into one 32-bit integer. Last 8 bytes: texture coordinates and which texture. The rule running through the whole codebase: the smaller each vertex, the more fit in cache, the faster the GPU chews through hundreds of thousands per frame.

The packed color integer — HSL, not RGB

The game stores color as HSL crammed into 16 bits — 6 bits hue, 3 saturation, 7 lightness — because it's a 2003 engine and that reproduces the original faceted, slightly-banded look. The shader converts HSL→RGB on the GPU, ported line-for-line from the original client. That middle integer: low 16 bits are HSL, next byte is a depth "bias," top byte is transparency. Three values in one int via shifts and masks; the shader pulls them back with mirror-image shifts. The UVs in the last chunk are shorts ×256 so fractions survive, plus a texture layer number — layer 0 means "no texture, just color."

One giant buffer, sliced

One big buffer, deliberately carved. The front is the static arena — terrain, walls, scenery — written once when a region loads. The rest is three dynamic arenas, one per frame-in-flight, each frame scribbling its moving stuff (players, NPCs) into its own slice. The three slices exist because of the exact race: the CPU writes frame six's monsters while the GPU reads frame four's. Give each in-flight frame its own slice and they physically cannot collide. Static-once, dynamic-times-three — the memory-layout expression of "three frames in flight."

Reverse-Z: the depth buffer is upside down on purpose

The clear value for depth is zero, not one — which looks like a bug. Normal depth: near=0, far=1, clear to 1, keep whatever's less. This codebase flips it: far=0, near=1, clear to 0, keep whatever's greater. Why? Floats have tons of precision near zero and little far out. In normal depth that precision bunches at the near plane where you don't need it and distant objects z-fight. Flip it and the dense precision lands where distant geometry is — z-fighting basically vanishes. Nearly free, strictly better. The "bias" byte touches depth too: divided down to a small number and added to z in the vertex shader, a per-vertex nudge so decals draw on top of what they sit on.

The sorter, for real

Not "sort back to front" — a bucket sort by depth, ported verbatim from the original client's face-priority sorter, copyright preserved, because matching the game matters more than cleverness. Per model: project every vertex; for each triangle do a back-face cull (cross-product of the three on-screen corners — facing away? throw it out, roughly half of any solid object, free). Survivors drop into buckets by depth (a linked list per distance value out to the model's diameter), then walk buckets farthest-to-nearest. Counting sort, not n-log-n.

  • The stamp trick — clearing the reused bucket arrays thousands of times a frame is wasteful, so a counter ticks up per model and each bucket records "the last stamp that touched me." Stale unless the stamp matches the current model. Never clear; just bump a number. One real clear when the counter wraps. Lazy invalidation.
  • Priority faces — the cape-over-body problem. Faces tagged 0–11 override pure depth. The sorter reproduces the original's exact interleaving (you'll see variables for the average of priorities 1&2, 3&4, 6&8). Not pretty; the comment says "this matches the client, do not make it elegant." Fidelity beats elegance.
  • Two outs — opaque-and-small models skip bucketing (cull-only path); any vertex closer than z=50 rejects the whole model (near-plane clip, the original's magic number).

Push constants: the tiny mailbox to the shaders

The fastest way to get a small amount of data to a shader — a couple hundred bytes stuffed right into the command buffer, no buffer, no descriptor, no allocation. Laid out by byte offset that code and shader must agree on exactly. First 64 bytes: the camera matrix. Then fog params (camera position, draw distance). Then misc ints (the current tick, for animating textures). Further along, the fragment shader's chunk: fog color and brightness. The favorite dirty trick: one float carries two values — it holds the smooth-banding setting (0–1) but also encodes the transparency pass by adding 10 or 20 on top. The shader integer-divides by ten to recover the pass, subtracts it back to recover the banding. Push-constant space is hard-limited (sometimes 128 bytes guaranteed), so you pack.

Inside the fragment shader

Runs once per pixel. First, discards: an "invisible" sentinel (transparency == 255) discards immediately; then, by transparency pass, discard either see-through or solid pixels so each pass handles its own kind. Then color, two branches: no texture → produce from HSL (smooth or sharp per-pixel decode by the banding setting); texture → sample the big array by UV and layer, throw away near-transparent pixels (that's how tree leaves get ragged edges instead of square cards), tint by the face's lightness. Three finishing moves in order: brightness (gamma power), fog (blend toward fog color), then color-blindness correction (matrix Daltonization) after fog so the fog gets corrected too. Out comes the pixel with alpha = 1 − transparency.

Alpha-to-coverage

Instead of true blending for every see-through face, the anti-aliasing hardware turns that alpha into a number of sub-pixel samples it lights up — half-transparent means roughly half the samples drawn. From normal viewing distance it reads as transparency, and you skip a whole expensive sorted blend pass. A cheap approximation that looks right.

Fences and semaphores, concretely

Three kinds of sync object, and the counts tell the story:

  • Fence per frame-in-flight (three) — the leash; before the CPU reuses slot one's notebook and memory it waits on slot one's fence.
  • "Image available" semaphore per frame (three) — the swapchain signals it when the next image is actually ready; the GPU waits before drawing.
  • "Render finished" semaphore per swapchain image, not per frame — present can outlive a frame slot; the same image can come back around before you've cycled three slots. Tie it to the image, not the frame, or you wait on a recycled semaphore. A classic Vulkan footgun: ask "what is the lifetime of the thing this guards?"

The loop: wait this frame's fence, reset it, acquire an image (signals image-available), record the notebook, submit ("wait image-available before drawing, signal render-finished when done, trip this frame's fence"), present ("wait render-finished before showing"), advance slot.

The macOS path has no semaphores at all

On macOS the entire frame uses zero semaphores — a consequence of bypassing the normal present path. Through MoltenVK the normal "present this image" call had timing problems (frames out of order, flicker). So the code reaches through MoltenVK via an extension to grab Metal's own command queue, asks the display layer for its next drawable directly, wraps the Metal texture as a Vulkan image, renders, and tells Metal to present on that same queue. Both render and present go on the same Metal queue, and Metal guarantees in-queue order — so the present is automatically after the render, no semaphore needed. The per-frame fence stays (the CPU still needs to know when to reuse the notebook). The Mac recycles ~3 drawables, each bringing back the same texture pointer, so a little cache keyed by pointer builds three Vulkan wrappers and reuses them until a resize.

The landmines (the best part)

The "LANDMINE" comments are tombstones for days someone lost — the cheapest education in the repo. Greatest hits:

  • Color format — two screen formats look identical: UNORM and SRGB. Pick SRGB and the GPU "corrects" colors with a gamma curve, but the art is already in display space, so it double-applies and the whole game gets ~twice as bright. Fix: prefer UNORM. One line, invisible until you notice everything looks bleached.
  • Teardown order — on AMD's open-source Linux driver, destroying a swapchain without first making the GPU finish makes the entire Java process silently exit — no crash, no stack trace. So "wait for GPU idle" calls are sprinkled before teardown. You own lifetimes now; "finish all GPU work before destroying what it used" is non-negotiable. OpenGL did this for you.
  • Present mode on Mac — "mailbox" mode through MoltenVK occasionally presents drawables out of order, flashing the previous frame's color. So the code forces plain locked-to-refresh mode on Mac unless uncapped. "Works everywhere except this one translation layer" is the entire personality of macOS support.
  • The honest one — a layer-flicker bug on Apple Silicon is still not fully nailed. Some landmines are fixes for cousins of it. Not pretending it's solved.

The abstraction earning its keep

Best example: the bind group — a bundle of textures and samplers a shader uses. Some textures stream (rewritten every frame, like the interface image). If all three frames pointed at one texture, frame six rewrites it while frame four reads — the flicker race again. So one logical bind group actually allocates three real descriptor sets, one per frame slot, each pointing at that slot's copy. The rendering code just says "bind this bind group" and the layer hands over the right slot's version. The same "three slices" idea a third time — you've now seen it in the vertex buffer, the sync objects, and the bind groups. Spot that one pattern and three-quarters of the codebase stops being mysterious.

Where to start reading tonight

Open the renderer's per-frame function and find the fence wait near the top — the one commenting on the interface layer covering and uncovering. Read it slowly. It contains, in one paragraph, the frame ordering, the three-frames-in-flight race, the streaming-texture problem, and a real unsolved bug. The whole project compressed into one defensive wait. Pull every thread it mentions. The scary comments are where the knowledge lives; the clean code is just the part that already worked.

The Handover: A Guided Tour of the Source

Real class names, real methods, real fields, in the order the code runs. Not theory — a tour you follow with the files open. When DEV names a class, it exists; when DEV names a field, grep it.

Episodes one and two covered what and the techniques. This one is our code, specifically — the spine, the engine callbacks, the scene capture and draw, the platform floor, and the landmine index. Pause it, open the file being discussed. That's the point.

🎧
Podcast · two voices · ~60 min
The Handover
DEV hands the project off and walks the actual code — class by class, method by method, in run order. Where a line is load-bearing or cursed, we stop and say why.

Notes

What owns what

One package, net.runelite.client.plugins.gpuvulkan, ~80 files plus one Objective-C file under src/main/native. The spine is GpuVulkanPlugin.java: class GpuVulkanPlugin extends Plugin implements DrawCallbacks, VulkanRenderBackend — three hats. It's a RuneLite Plugin (startUp/shutDown), a DrawCallbacks (the engine calls it to draw the world), and a VulkanRenderBackend (other plugins register render extensions). Four fields decode the rest: renderer (a VulkanRenderer), renderExtensions (the things that draw), disposables (a LIFO close-stack — everything created is pushed and torn down in reverse), and two static volatile fields (activeInstance, shuttingDown) for the JVM shutdown hook.

startUp, and why construction order is sacred

startUp does almost nothing synchronously: checks isStockGpuEnabled() (refuse if the stock OpenGL plugin is on — two owners of the rlawt context crash the JVM), registers the shutdown hook, sets startRequested = true, defers the real work to clientThread.invoke until logged in (attaching on the login screen leaves a blank canvas indistinguishable from a broken surface, especially on macOS). Then a specific order encoded in Disposables: VulkanInstance → VulkanSurface → VulkanDevice, then — LANDMINE — FrameSync is registered before Swapchain. Disposables is LIFO, so at teardown the swapchain destroys first (releasing the windowing system's references to the renderFinished semaphores) and only then the semaphores. The other way, AMD's RADV hangs in vkDestroySemaphore; vkDeviceWaitIdle does not drain the presentation engine.

Then MSAA sample count (macOS forces samples=1 — MSAA on the custom-present path is disabled while a MoltenVK submit crash is isolated), DepthBuffer, optional MsaaColorBuffer, RenderPass, Gfx.wrap, TextureArray, RegionManager, RenderExtensions (immediately register(new BaseRenderer())), Framebuffers, VulkanRenderer. Last steps in order: activeInstance = this after full init (so the shutdown hook never sees a half-built plugin), then client.setDrawCallbacks(this) (the switch that starts the engine calling us). The giant catch (RuntimeException) must unwind everything in the same order shutDown would, or the canvas is left with ignoreRepaint=true and a half-attached context — which presents as "the plugin won't turn back on."

The DrawCallbacks contract — how the engine feeds us

The engine's calling pattern in this client version is not what the interface docs imply:

  • preSceneDraw fires first each frame — stash camera floats into lastCamX etc. (build the projection from these, not the doubles from drawScene); detect scene transitions by scene != capturedScene by reference (the swapScene/loadScene callbacks don't fire in this version) → captureSceneNow; set scene.setDrawDistance so clickboxes register; forward hideRoofIds.
  • drawScene kicks off per-frame capture: beginFrame(), rebuildDirtyZones, captureDynamicPending(). Does not use the camera doubles it's passed.
  • Three model entry points funnel into captureModel(...): drawDynamic, drawTemp, and draw(Projection, …) — the last has the most honest comment in the codebase: projectiles, spell animations, and home-teleport stop rendering without that method body even though the counter says it never runs. Issue #1. Leave it.
  • draw(int overlayColor) is the heartbeat — the engine saying "now paint it." Validates canvas, handles resize, grabs UI pixels, calls renderer.drawFrame(...) with the whole pile of state (tick masked & 127 to stop float drift). That one call is the entire frame.

The extension fan-out

RenderExtensions is a List<VulkanRenderExtension> with crash isolation — forEachExtension wraps each callback in try/catch and a throwing extension is removed and closed while the rest keep running. Registered by default: BaseRenderer, a thin dispatcher to a scene renderer and an interface renderer, gated by benchmark flags. Below it: VulkanSceneRenderer → DefaultVulkanSceneRenderer (near pass-through) → SceneRenderer. The routing that matters: captureModel overloads carrying a Projection go to captureModelSorted; the bare one goes unsorted. Third-party plugins use VulkanExtensionQueue — register before the backend is live, attachQueued wires them once renderExtensions exists.

SceneRenderer part one — the buffer and static capture

The biggest file and the heart — implements three sink interfaces because it owns the vertex memory. Constants: MAX_STATIC_VERTICES = 5,000,000, MAX_FRAME_VERTICES = 3,000,000, TOTAL_BUFFER_BYTES = STATIC + FRAME × FRAMES_IN_FLIGHT. One host-visible buffer: a 5M-vertex static arena at the front, then three 3M-vertex frame arenas. Each vertex is VERTEX_STRIDE = 20 bytes. The cursor is three fields (writePtr, writeBasePtr, writeBaseVertex); useStaticWriteArena() and useFrameWriteArena(slot, …) flip between arenas; everything writes via SceneVertexPacker.writePacked and advances 20.

The world grid: ZONE_SIZE = 8, ZONES_PER_SIDE = 13, ZONE_COUNT = 169. Bookkeeping arrays zoneVertexStart[layer][plane][zone] / zoneVertexCount record which slice of the static arena holds each zone's geometry — the entire culling and roof-hiding mechanism. captureScene runs on a region change: first line vkDeviceWaitIdle (drain the GPU before rewriting the shared buffer), zero the bookkeeping, useStaticWriteArena(), emit five layers in fixed order (TERRAIN, WALLS, DECORATIVE, GROUND, GAME_OBJECTS — DYNAMIC is never static). Layer-major, plane-minor, so roof-hiding clips per plane. SceneRoofInfo.forTile returns a visbelow flag and roofId; level 0 holds its own geometry plus upper levels' "visible below," mirroring stock's SceneUploader. roofOffset = (EXTENDED 184 − SCENE 104)/2 on top-level, 0 on instances — threaded as a parameter so it's impossible to forget.

SceneRenderer part two — models, tiles, dirty zones, dynamic suffix

  • Models via SceneModelEmitter. captureModelSorted decides the path: consult ModelFaceCache for transparent-face count; if RENDERMODE_SORTED_NO_DEPTH force a sort; if opaque and under OPAQUE_UNSORTED_FACE_THRESHOLD (64) go unsorted; if transparent try the cull-only fused path; else fall back to ModelSorter — the bucket sort from episode two, ported from stock's FacePrioritySorter, near-plane-rejecting at z=50.
  • Priority faces recorded in PriorityRangeSet; recordDraw later draws those ranges through color then depth pipelines.
  • Tiles via SceneTileEmitter — captureTilePaint builds a 128-unit quad, six writeHslVert calls, texture layer = paint.getTexture() + 1 (layer 0 is white reserve). Shader does HSL→RGB.
  • beginFrame holds the safety wait — vkWaitForFences on this slot's fence before touching the arena, or the CPU writes while the GPU reads three frames ago (torn-vertex flicker, worse on macOS). Resets vertexCount to overlayNextVertex[slot].
  • Dirty zones — DirtyZoneTracker keeps a per-zone bitmask of which of the three slots have been rebuilt; a zone is clean only when all slots are done, because each slot is a separate copy.
  • PendingRenderables — a retry queue for models whose data hasn't streamed in yet; captureDynamicPending retries each frame.
  • drawPass(PASS_OPAQUE) records dynamicOpaqueEnd so opaque dynamic geometry draws in the main pass, the rest in the alpha pass.

VulkanRenderer.drawFrame — the frame start to finish

Stashes incoming state (the matrix is built later at record time). First real action inside the MemoryStack push is a fence wait — the comment to read: wait this slot's in-flight fence before writing the UI staging buffer, because the memcpy in uploadUiPixels can race the GPU's copy from three frames ago, producing a half-old/half-new UI texture that reads as "the UI layer intermittently covering and uncovering" — the layer-flicker family. Then branch: drawFrameCustomPresent (macOS) or drawFrameSwapchain (else), then sync.advance().

Swapchain path: vkAcquireNextImageKHR with imageAvailable; on OUT_OF_DATE/SUBOPTIMAL mark stale; reset the fence; record via recordClearPass; submit (waits imageAvailable at COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OUTPUT, signals renderFinishedFor(imageIdx) — per image, trips the fence); present. recordClearPass: recordBeforeRenderPass (UI uploads and layout transitions outside the render pass — vkCmdCopyBufferToImage is illegal inside), color cleared to skybox, depth cleared to 0.0 (reverse-Z). Two viewport landmines: DPI stretch from canvas dims not viewport dims (else clicks land in shifting spots), and the Y-flip baked into the matrix as scale(scale, -scale, 1) (the KHR_maintenance1 negative-height viewport was silently ignored at runtime).

macOS custom-present path: MacOSMetalHelper.nextDrawable() returns drawable/MTLTexture pointers and size; on size change vkDeviceWaitIdle, flush the MetalDrawableSet cache, recreate depth/MSAA; metalDrawables.acquire imports the MTLTexture as a VkImage via VkImportMetalTextureInfoEXT, cached by texture pointer; record the clear pass; submitNoSemaphores (no wait/signal — present on the same Metal queue, in-queue order sequences for free); wait the fence; presentDrawable on the queue pulled once at device creation via vkExportMetalObjectsEXT.

SceneRenderer.recordDraw — how draws get issued

The busiest method. Bail if vertexCount == 0. LANDMINE: bind the vertex buffer at byte offset zero and address the per-slot arena via firstVertex, never a byte offset — MoltenVK's offset translation produces no visible geometry at hundreds-of-MB offsets on Apple Silicon. Push constants: vertex push 96 bytes (mat4 MVP, then a vec4 of camera/draw-distance/fog, then an ivec4 tick); fragment push 32 bytes at offset 96 (fog+brightness, then a vec4 with the two-values-in-one-float trick: singlePassAlpha ? 20 + smoothBanding : smoothBanding, alpha pass uses 10 + smoothBanding). roofRanges.buildSkipPairs(hideRoofIds, …) writes [start, end) skip pairs for hidden roofs. A visible-zone window culls by zone box unless the radius covers the whole scene.

A loop over six layers picks a pipeline (line if wireframe and supported, else fill), binds it and the descriptor set. DYNAMIC draws the dynamic range honoring priority skip-pairs; static layers draw plane-by-plane via zoneDrawScheduler.drawStaticPlane/drawOverlayPlane, applying roof skip-pairs only for planes above the current one (so a building you're inside doesn't lose its lower walls). SceneZoneDrawScheduler coalesces contiguous zone ranges, merges roof + overlay skips via mergeSkipPairs; SceneDrawEmitter.drawRange walks the sorted skip-pairs and issues a vkCmdDraw for each gap — roof-hiding as gaps in the draw list, no separate geometry. After the loop: priority ranges (color then depth pipeline), and unless single-pass alpha, an alphaPipeline pass. repushConstantsEveryDraw = supportsMetalObjects() — on Mac re-push every draw (MoltenVK push-constant reuse quirks).

The Gfx layer and the UI

The scene path uses raw Vulkan and ScenePipeline directly — it's hot and predates the abstraction. The gfx package (wgpu-style) is used by the UI and FSR; Gfx.wrap returns a GfxRenderer that doesn't own the objects it wraps. InterfaceRenderer owns a GfxStreamingImage (a ring of FRAMES_IN_FLIGHT textures + staging) and a GfxBindGroup: uploadUiPixels memcpys AWT pixels into the current slot's staging (optional dirty-row path via Arrays.mismatch); recordBeforeRenderPass does the copy and transitions; in-pass it draws a fullscreen triangle (no vertex buffer — gl_VertexIndex makes the corners), and ui.frag mixes in overlayColor (login fade, hit-splat flash). A single logical GfxBindGroup allocates FRAMES_IN_FLIGHT descriptor sets so the GPU never samples the slot the CPU is writing — triple-buffering real but invisible.

The platform floor

  • VulkanInstance — filters requested extensions against available (on macOS VK_KHR_portability_enumeration may not be advertised); wires a validation debug messenger to slf4j; sets the ENUMERATE_PORTABILITY flag or vkCreateInstance returns ERROR_INCOMPATIBLE_DRIVER on Mac.
  • VulkanDevice — picks the first device with a single graphics+present queue; reads feature flags; enables VK_KHR_swapchain always, VK_KHR_portability_subset if advertised (mandatory on MoltenVK), VK_EXT_metal_objects if present. extractMetalCommandQueue() via vkExportMetalObjectsEXT pulls the id<MTLCommandQueue>. supportsMetalObjects() is the single flag that flips the renderer onto the macOS custom-present path.
  • PlatformSurface.current — X11PlatformSurface (Xlib surface, exposes currentDrawable so the plugin recreates on X11 drawable swaps), Win32PlatformSurface (HWND — implemented, never tested, issue #4), MacOSPlatformSurface (attachMetalLayer, then memPutAddress to write the layer pointer manually because LWJGL 3.3.6's pLayer binding has a bug).
  • rlmtl.m → librlmtl.dylib, System.loaded by MacOSMetalHelper. Creates a CAMetalLayer (BGRA8Unorm, drawableSize 1×1 initially to avoid colliding with MoltenVK's internal swapchain) on the main thread, spins a CADisplayLink to sync drawableSize and flush CATransaction. nNextDrawable/nPresentDrawable wrapped in @autoreleasepool — mandatory, or MoltenVK crashes around frame fifty.
  • ClientRuntimeConfig — applies engine settings on enable (GPU flags, expanded map, unlocked FPS) and restores on disable.

Render pass, attachments, textures, FSR

RenderPass(device, colorFormat, samples, swapchainPresent) — with MSAA three attachments (multisampled color, multisampled depth with reverse-Z so compare is GREATER and clear is 0, and a resolve target); the swapchainPresent boolean sets the color final layout (PRESENT_SRC_KHR normally, COLOR_ATTACHMENT_OPTIMAL for macOS custom present). Attachment order in Framebuffers must match exactly. TextureArray — a 2D array, 128×128, 8 mips, layer 0 solid white; the landmine is the sampler magFilter = NEAREST not LINEAR (OSRS encodes alpha as rgb==0 and the shader discards below 0.5; LINEAR would blend transparent texels into neighbors and break wall transparency). minFilter is LINEAR. Builds the animation UBO (256 std140 vec4, per-tick UV scroll for water/lava). FsrUpscaler — only when upscaling is FSR1 and renderScale < 100: EASU then RCAS fullscreen-triangle passes on the gfx abstraction.

The landmine index, and the open wounds

Grep LANDMINE. By blast radius: (1) register FrameSync before Swapchain or RADV hangs on teardown; (2) pickFormat must prefer B8G8R8A8_UNORM not SRGB or the game doubles in brightness; (3) double vkDeviceWaitIdle around swapchain destroy (RADV silent exit + MoltenVK artifacts); (4) bind vertex buffer at offset zero, shift with firstVertex, or MoltenVK renders nothing; (5) scene viewport DPI from canvas dims not viewport dims or clicks land wrong; (6) texture sampler mag filter NEAREST or wall transparency breaks; (7) every rlmtl.m native entry needs @autoreleasepool or MoltenVK crashes around frame fifty. Open wounds: the macOS layer-flicker bug (read CLAUDE.md's running notes — many plausible fixes already ruled out), the draw(Projection, …) method that "never runs" but breaks projectiles (issue #1), and untested Windows surface code (issue #4).

Build, run, and the house rules

Needs JDK 21 Temurin and glslangValidator on PATH (Gradle compiles shaders to SPIR-V). ./gradlew build. Run via GpuVulkanPluginTest#main, ./gradlew run, or ./gradlew shadowJar for a ~41MB standalone jar (bundles a RuneLite client plus LWJGL natives including MoltenVK) — run with -ea. The rules are scar tissue: a failed fix is data (if a change doesn't fix the symptom, your causal model is wrong — pick a different hypothesis family, don't stack plausible Vulkan fixes); don't diagnose by adding logging and pasting output — derive from the code or use validation and RenderDoc; silent validation under the failure means the bug is not in Vulkan API usage (look in MoltenVK translation, AWT, present timing, or shader semantics); correlated symptoms share a cause; structural changes need buy-in; no upstream-incompatible hacks. Commits use conventional-commit style with an Assisted-by: trailer where an assistant materially shaped the code — never Co-Authored-By:.

The sentence taped to the monitor: "Follow the data from draw, read the scary comments first, and when a fix doesn't work, change your model instead of changing a different line."
